Ask any student how they use AI and you will hear a long list: explaining a hard concept, checking grammar, brainstorming a project, summarising a chapter, practising for an exam, and sometimes doing the homework outright. AI arrived in education not through a policy decision but through students' phones.

The institutions responsible for learning are still catching up.

## The guidance gap, in numbers

- In 2023, UNESCO found that **fewer than 10% of schools and universities** it surveyed had formal guidance on generative AI.
- In 2026, a Gallup and Walton Family Foundation survey of **2,069 US teachers**, run in February and March, found that **82% had received no formal guidance** on AI use from their school or district. Teachers described the lack of guidance as a major problem.
- At UNESCO's **Digital Learning Week in September 2026**, education ministers adopted a statement calling for **human-centred rules** as AI reshapes education, putting teachers, learners, equity and safety at the centre.

The gap is clear: AI use is nearly universal, and clear rules are still the exception.

## Why it matters

Without guidance, every teacher makes up their own rules, and students get mixed messages from one class to the next. One teacher bans AI completely; another encourages it; a third never mentions it. Meanwhile:

- **Learning can be hollowed out.** If an assistant does the thinking, the student gets the grade but not the skill.
- **Assessment gets unreliable.** Take-home essays and problem sets no longer show what a student can do alone.
- **Privacy is at risk.** Students may paste personal details, or their classmates', into tools whose data practices nobody has checked.
- **Inequality can grow.** Students with good devices, paid tools and support at home pull further ahead.
- **Mistakes go unnoticed.** AI tools can be confidently wrong, and students who can't yet judge the answer won't notice.

## What good guidance covers

The most useful school and university AI policies are short, practical and revisited often. They usually answer six questions:

1. **What's allowed, and when?** Many schools use simple levels per assignment: *no AI*, *AI for ideas and feedback only*, or *AI allowed and acknowledged*.
2. **How should AI help be acknowledged?** A short note on which tool was used, for what, and how its output was checked.
3. **Which tools are approved?** Chosen for privacy, age-appropriateness and data protection, not just popularity.
4. **What data must never be shared?** Personal details, grades, health information and other students' work.
5. **How will assessment adapt?** More in-class work, oral explanations, drafts and process portfolios, which show how a student got to an answer.
6. **How will teachers be supported?** Training, time to experiment, and a place to share what works.

## Learning by doing beats learning by asking

There is a deeper point here. When an answer to almost any factual question is one prompt away, the value of education shifts from *knowing* to *doing*: framing a problem, testing ideas, building something, noticing when it breaks and fixing it.

Project-based learning fits this moment well. When students build a working app, run an experiment or solve a real problem for a real community, AI becomes a tool in a bigger process rather than a shortcut around it. The student still has to decide what to build, judge whether the AI's suggestion is right, and explain the result.

This is the approach we believe in at Beyondlogik: practical knowledge, industry-oriented challenges and project-based experiences, where curiosity turns into something real.

## Advice for students right now

Even without a school policy, students can use AI in ways that build skills instead of replacing them:

- **Ask it to explain, not to answer.** "Explain this step by step and give me a similar problem to try" teaches more than "solve this".
- **Try first, then compare.** Attempt the problem on your own, then use AI to check and explain the gaps.
- **Verify everything.** Check facts, sources and calculations, especially anything you'll submit.
- **Say how you used it.** Being open about AI help builds trust with teachers.
- **Protect your data.** Don't paste personal or other people's information into AI tools.

## The takeaway

AI in education isn't a future question. It's in every classroom already, mostly unmanaged. The fix isn't a blanket ban or blind enthusiasm. It's clear, human-centred guidance and teaching that puts doing, building and thinking at the centre, so students learn to use AI well rather than lean on it.
