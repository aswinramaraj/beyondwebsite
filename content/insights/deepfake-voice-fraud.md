For years, security training taught people to distrust emails: check the sender, hover over links, look for spelling mistakes. Attackers have adapted. In 2026, one of the most effective attacks is a phone call, in a voice you recognise, asking for something urgent.

## The numbers behind the trend

- Mandiant's *M-Trends 2026* report found that **voice phishing ("vishing") was the initial way in for 11% of the intrusions it investigated**, making it the second most common entry point. Email phishing accounted for 6%.
- The FBI's latest Internet Crime Report added a dedicated section on AI-enabled fraud. Analyses of the report put **AI-related losses in 2025 at around $893 million**.
- In a KnowBe4 survey, **53% of organisations named AI-powered phishing as their top AI-enabled fraud concern**.
- Hiya, a call-protection company, found that **31% of consumers had received a deepfake voice call**.

JPMorgan Chase's technology team put it plainly: AI is making social engineering easier than ever.

## How voice cloning works

Modern speech models can copy a voice from a short sample: a conference talk, a podcast, a social media video or even a brief phone call. The attacker types what they want to say and the model speaks it in the target's voice, with their accent and rhythm. Some tools can do this live, during a conversation.

The voice doesn't have to be perfect. Phone audio is compressed and noisy, and the listener is busy. "Close enough", combined with urgency and authority, is usually enough.

## The scams you're most likely to see

**The urgent payment.** Someone who sounds like a senior executive calls finance: a confidential deal is closing and a transfer must go out today. Sometimes it's a video call with several "colleagues", all generated.

**The help-desk reset.** The attacker calls IT pretending to be an employee locked out of their account and asks for a password reset or a new multi-factor device. Once that's done, they're in.

**The family emergency.** A parent gets a call from a crying "child" who has been in an accident and needs money immediately.

**The supplier change.** A "vendor" calls to confirm new bank details for future invoices.

All four rely on the same three levers: a trusted identity, time pressure and a request to skip the normal process.

## Why detection alone won't save you

Deepfake detectors exist and they are improving, but attackers improve too, and a phone call gives a detector very little to work with. Most experts agree that the most reliable defence is not spotting the fake. It is a **process that doesn't depend on recognising a voice at all**.

## Defences that actually work

**For organisations:**

- **Call back on a known number.** Any request involving money, credentials or data gets verified by calling the person back on a number from the company directory, never one supplied during the call.
- **Two people for payments.** Transfers above a threshold, and any change to a supplier's bank details, need approval from a second person through a separate channel.
- **Harden the help desk.** Password and MFA resets require strong identity checks, such as verification through a manager or an in-person or video check against an ID, not just answers to personal questions.
- **Use phishing-resistant sign-in.** Passkeys and hardware security keys can't be talked out of someone over the phone the way a one-time code can.
- **Train with real examples.** Let staff hear what a cloned voice sounds like. People who have heard one are much quicker to pause.
- **Make it safe to say no.** Executives should state clearly, in advance, that they will never mind being called back to confirm a request.

**For individuals and families:**

- **Agree on a family code word** to use in a real emergency.
- **Hang up and call back** on the number you already have saved.
- **Slow down.** Urgency is the attacker's main tool. A real emergency can survive a two-minute check.
- **Limit public voice samples** where you reasonably can, and be careful what you say to unknown callers who seem to want you to talk.

## The takeaway

The most dangerous thing about voice deepfakes isn't the technology. It's that they break a rule we've relied on for our whole lives: if it sounds like them, it is them. The fix is to replace that instinct with a habit. Any unexpected request for money or access, from any voice, gets verified through a channel you choose.
